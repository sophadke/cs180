Read Me · From Noise to an Image

Part A uses the pretrained PixNerd-XXL-P16-T2I model to explore how a flow model turns noise into an image. There is no training here: the work is in constructing noisy inputs, interpreting the predicted velocity, and choosing how to combine those predictions while sampling.

I use my brick-building photograph for Sections 1.1–1.5. Its repeated window frames, brick texture, and carved facade give the denoisers several kinds of detail to reconstruct. The later editing experiments add my yellow-notebook photograph, a web painting, and two drawings.

1.1 Constructing Noisy Training Inputs

I center-crop the input to a square, resize to 512 × 512, and normalize RGB to [−1, 1]. I sample one Gaussian noise tensor ε with seed 180 and reuse that exact tensor at all three times, so the comparisons change the mixing ratio rather than the noise realization.

xt = (1 − t)ε + tx1

Implementation · interpolate
def interpolate(image, noise, t):
    return (1 - t) * noise + t * image

At t = 0.25 the facade is barely visible behind the noise. The repeated window grid becomes readable at t = 0.50, and by t = 0.75 the sunlit brick and ornament are visible even though fine grain remains. Reusing one noise tensor makes that progression a comparison of signal strength rather than different noise patterns.

1.2 Classical Denoising

Gaussian blur is the baseline. Since the clean image is multiplied by t in the noisy input, I first divide by t to restore its amplitude, then blur. That division also amplifies the noise: its standard deviation becomes (1 − t)/t.

x̂1 = Gσ * (xt / t)

I compare σ ∈ {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} and select the value with the lowest full-image MSE against the original, before display clipping. This gives the blur baseline an oracle choice of σ. It is useful for comparison, but assumes a clean reference is available.

candidates = {s: gaussian_blur(x/t, [int(6*s+1)]*2, [s,s])
              for s in [1.,2.,4.,8.,16.]}
sigma = min(candidates,
            key=lambda s: ((candidates[s]-image)**2).mean().item())

At t = 0.25 the selected σ = 8 blur restores broad light and dark regions but turns the carved details and brickwork into soft color patches. The same operation that averages away noise also averages away real texture, so clean edges cannot be recovered just by increasing the blur.

1.3 One-Step Denoising

The model predicts a velocity, not a noise image. To estimate the clean endpoint from one prediction, I advance along that velocity for the remaining time 1 − t. All three estimates use the prompt a high quality photo and the noisy tensors saved in Section 1.1.

x̂1 = xt + (1 − t)vθ(xt, t, c)

Implementation · one_step_denoise
def one_step_denoise(x, t, condition, predict_velocity):
    v = predict_velocity(x, t, condition)
    return x + (1 - t) * v

One model prediction recovers the overall building even from t = 0.25, but the facade becomes unnaturally smooth: horizontal surface detail and carved ornament are lost. At t = 0.75 the estimate retains much more brick texture and sharper window frames. A plausible building is easier to reconstruct than the exact fine detail in the original.

1.4 Visualizing Velocity

The update added by one-step denoising is (1 − t)v̂. Because the clean image and sampled noise are both known, the exact path velocity is x1 − ε and the target update is (1 − t)(x1 − ε). I compare these raw updates before clipping or display scaling.

v_gt = clean_image - epsilon
predicted_update = (1 - t) * v_hat
ground_truth_update = (1 - t) * v_gt

1.5 Iterative Denoising with Euler Sampling

A one-step estimate treats the current direction as if it stays constant until t = 1. Euler sampling instead advances a little, predicts velocity at the new image and time, and repeats. Starting at the saved t = 0.50 noisy image, h = 0.02 gives exactly 25 updates.

xt+h = xt + h vθ(xt, t, c)

Implementation · euler_sample
def euler_sample(x_start, t_start, step_size, velocity):
    n_steps = math.ceil(round((1 - t_start) / step_size, 9))
    x = x_start
    states = [(float(t_start), x)]
    for k in range(n_steps):
        t = t_start + k * step_size              # computed directly, so error doesn't accumulate
        last = k == n_steps - 1
        h = (1 - t) if last else step_size       # shorten the final step to land exactly on t = 1
        x = x + h * velocity(x, t)               # recompute velocity at the current state every step
        t_next = 1.0 if last else t_start + (k + 1) * step_size
        if (k + 1) % 5 == 0 or last:
            states.append((t_next, x))
    return x, states

Numerical check. A constant velocity of 1 should move a zero state by 1 − tstart. The notebook checks 50 steps from 0, 25 from 0.50, and four from 0.93; the final 0.01 step is shortened to end exactly at 1.

The same t = 0.50 input produces three visibly different results. Gaussian blur leaves colored grain and soft edges; one-step denoising removes that grain but smooths the brick and decorative band. Euler sampling restores a much more textured facade and sharper ledges, closer to the original. Recomputing the direction after each small step helps recover detail that a single extrapolation misses. The measured MSE is 0.044655 for blur, 0.010660 for one-step, and 0.012645 for Euler. One-step is therefore closer in pixel error, despite Euler looking more textured; added detail is not necessarily the original detail.

1.6 Generating Images from Noise

To generate rather than reconstruct, I start at t = 0 with five independent Gaussian noise tensors. Each is integrated for 50 Euler updates under a high quality photo. Seeds 180–184 are kept for the paired CFG comparison in the next section.

for i in range(5):
    x = resumable_sample(f"conditional_{i}", noise(SEED+i), 0,
        lambda x,t: predict_velocity(x,t,condition))

All five samples from the generic prompt are portraits, rather than a diverse collection of objects or landscapes. Some are coherent, but seed 180 has severely malformed facial features, and seeds 181 and 183 contain obvious anatomical or texture artifacts. A broad prompt exposes a strong portrait bias in this particular set of samples.

2.1 Classifier-Free Guidance

Classifier-free guidance evaluates the same noisy image and time twice: once with the text condition and once with an empty-string condition. Their difference points toward features favored by the prompt. I add seven copies of that difference to the conditional velocity.

vCFG = vc + w(vc − vu),   w = 7

Implementation · cfg_velocity
def cfg_velocity(x, t, condition, w, predict_velocity, null_condition=''):
    v_c = predict_velocity(x, t, condition)
    if w == 0:
        return v_c  # w = 0 is plain conditional prediction; skip the unconditional pass
    v_u = predict_velocity(x, t, null_condition)
    return v_c + w * (v_c - v_u)

The notebook checks that w = 0 returns the ordinary conditional prediction. The five samples below reuse the same initial noise tensors as Section 1.6, so each seed forms a controlled comparison.

With the same five seeds, CFG produces much cleaner, sharper portrait photographs. Seed 180 is now a coherent face instead of the distorted head in the unguided result. The background and lighting also become more consistent. Guidance improves visual coherence here, but the set still consists entirely of portraits; it does not remove the subject bias of the generic prompt.

3.1 Image-to-Image Editing

Image editing starts between noise and the input image, then integrates back toward t = 1 with the generic photo prompt. Every sweep uses tstart ∈ {0.02, 0.04, 0.06, 0.10, 0.14, 0.25, 0.50}, h = 0.02, and w = 7. Reusing seed 180 holds the noise realization fixed across each sweep.

Implementation · edit
def edit(image, noise, t_start, condition, predict_velocity, w=7, step_size=0.02):
    x = interpolate(image, noise, t_start)
    v = lambda x, t: cfg_velocity(x, t, condition, w, predict_velocity, '')
    out, _ = euler_sample(x, t_start, step_size, v)
    return out

At low starting times, the input contributes little beyond broad spatial or color cues; at higher starting times, more of the original structure remains. The six inputs deliberately include photographs, a painting, linework, and watercolor.

3.3 Visual Anagrams

An anagram asks one image to satisfy two different views. At every step, I predict CFG velocity for prompt A in the upright view. I also rotate the state 180°, predict with prompt B, rotate that velocity back, and average the two. Both directions then contribute to the same Euler update.

v = ½[vCFG(x,t,cA) + F(vCFG(F(x),t,cB))]

Implementation · visual_anagrams
def visual_anagrams(noise, condition_a, condition_b, predict_velocity, w=7, step_size=0.02):
    def v(x, t):
        v1 = cfg_velocity(x, t, condition_a, w, predict_velocity, '')
        v2 = rotate180(cfg_velocity(rotate180(x), t, condition_b, w, predict_velocity, ''))
        return (v1 + v2) / 2
    out, _ = euler_sample(noise, 0, step_size, v)
    return out

F flips both spatial axes, rather than only reflecting the image vertically. The notebook verifies F(F(x)) = x. The two columns below show the same output upright and rotated, with its two prompts.

The first output reads clearly as a dark-and-white dog upright. Rotating it turns the pale fur into pointed snowy ridges and the dark patches into a mountain valley, although some dog-face structure is still visible. The rabbit is also clear in the second output. Rotated, the orange shapes suggest a ship and sails, but the eye remains conspicuous, making this second illusion less convincing. Averaging velocities balances two prompts; it does not guarantee equally strong interpretations.

3.4 Hybrid Images

For a hybrid, the two prompts act on different spatial frequencies rather than different orientations. Prompt A supplies the blurred, low-frequency velocity; prompt B supplies the residual high-frequency velocity. Their sum drives each Euler update.

v = LP(vA) + [vB − LP(vB)]

Implementation · make_hybrids
def make_hybrids(noise, condition_a, condition_b, predict_velocity, w=7, step_size=0.02):
    def v(x, t):
        v_a = cfg_velocity(x, t, condition_a, w, predict_velocity, '')
        v_b = cfg_velocity(x, t, condition_b, w, predict_velocity, '')
        return lowpass(v_a) + (v_b - lowpass(v_b))
    out, _ = euler_sample(noise, 0, step_size, v)
    return out

The low-pass filter is Gaussian, with a 265 × 265 kernel and σ = 16 at 512 × 512. The near view preserves the fine detail; reducing the same image makes its coarse structure easier to see. Each row uses one saved image at two display sizes.

The reduced views retain an oval composition and a face-like arrangement of shapes, but the intended lion and owl are not clearly recognizable. These runs demonstrate the frequency-combination procedure, but their low-frequency subjects are weak. A successful numerical run and a successful perceptual hybrid are different criteria.

What I Learned

I learned that a flow model predicts a direction of change, rather than simply identifying noise to subtract. Taking small Euler steps lets me update that direction as the image changes. On my building photo, this recovered sharper window frames and more brick texture than a single prediction, while Gaussian blur removed detail along with the noise.

I also learned that a sharper-looking reconstruction is not necessarily more accurate. One-step denoising had a lower MSE (0.010660) than Euler sampling (0.012645), even though the Euler result looked more detailed. That comparison showed me that the model can invent plausible texture instead of recovering the exact pixels in my photograph.

The editing sweeps helped me understand how starting time controls the balance between the input and the model. At very low times, my building and yellow notebook sometimes became portraits because almost none of the original image survived. Higher starting times preserved their structure. Specific prompts gave me much more control: I could turn the Campanile into a lighthouse, add vines to the facade, or change the notebook cover toward red, but the edits became weaker as I preserved more of the source.

I saw a similar tradeoff with classifier-free guidance. Reusing the same five noise seeds made the comparison clear: guidance produced more coherent, sharper portraits, but it did not remove the portrait bias of the generic prompt. Better-looking samples did not automatically mean a wider range of subjects.

Finally, I learned that combining velocity predictions is easier than making both visual interpretations convincing. The dog/mountain anagram worked more clearly than the rabbit/ship, and my hybrids showed the mountain ridges and leaves more strongly than the intended distant lion and owl. I would try more prompt pairs and seeds to improve those results. The main lesson I took from this project is to check the equations, the measurements, and the actual images together: each reveals something the others can miss.

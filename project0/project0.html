Becoming Friends with Your Camera

by Sohum Phadke  /  Fall 2026

Read Me

In this project, I explored the relationship between perspective, focal length/zoom, and the center of projection. Every camera makes two decisions at once: how far it stands from the world, and how much it magnifies what it sees. Change them together and a photo can stay the same size but tell a completely different story about depth. The three experiments below isolate that relationship — first on a face, then on a building, then in one continuous move known as the dolly zoom.

Part 1 — The Selfie: Wrong Way vs. Right Way

close-up.jpg vs. stepped-back-zoomed.jpg

Moving the camera from a few inches away to several feet away — while zooming in to match the framing — changes how the nose, ears, and cheeks scale relative to each other. In the close-up, the nose sits noticeably closer to the lens than the ears do, and at that short distance the small difference in depth across the face becomes a large difference in relative size: the nose balloons, the ears shrink and edge out of frame, and the whole face reads as rounder and more bulging. Stepping back and zooming in makes the nose-to-lens and ear-to-lens distances nearly equal, so the angular size of each feature normalizes and the face looks flatter and more proportional — closer to how it actually looks to someone standing a normal conversational distance away. This is the same reason portrait photographers favor longer lenses shot from farther back rather than wide lenses up close.

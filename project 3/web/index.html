Part 1: Fun with Filters

1.1 Convolutions from Scratch

Convolution slides a small filter over the image. At every output pixel it multiplies the filter against the pixel's neighborhood and adds the products. Before sliding, the filter is flipped 180° (that flip is what makes it convolution rather than correlation). To keep the output the same size as the input, the image is padded with zeros on every side by half the filter size.

The four-loop version loops over every output pixel (i, j) and, inside that, over every filter entry (u, v):

def conv_four_loops(img, kernel):
    kernel = kernel[::-1, ::-1]                 # flip
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    ph, pw = kh // 2, kw // 2
    padded = np.pad(img, ((ph, ph), (pw, pw)))   # zero padding
    H, W = img.shape
    out = np.zeros((H, W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            total = 0
            for u in range(kh):
                for v in range(kw):
                    total += padded[i + u, j + v] * kernel[u, v]
            out[i, j] = total
    return out

The two-loop version replaces the inner loops with one numpy step: slice out the whole neighborhood, multiply element-wise, and sum.

def conv_two_loops(img, kernel):
    kernel = kernel[::-1, ::-1]
    kh, kw = kernel.shape
    padded = np.pad(img, ((kh // 2, (kh - 1) // 2), (kw // 2, (kw - 1) // 2)))
    H, W = img.shape
    out = np.zeros((H, W))
    for i in range(H):
        for j in range(W):
            patch = padded[i:i + kh, j:j + kw]
            out[i, j] = np.sum(patch * kernel)
    return out

Both match scipy.signal.convolve2d(img, k, mode="same", boundary="fill") to floating-point precision. Runtimes on my photo:

Runtime. scipy is hundreds of times faster than either of mine because it runs compiled code instead of Python loops. For the 9×9 box filter, two loops beat four loops, since each pixel's 81 multiply-adds happen in one numpy call. For the tiny 2-entry Dx/Dy filters, the four-loop version can actually be faster: the numpy slicing and np.sum overhead per pixel costs more than two plain Python multiplications.

Boundaries. My functions pad with zeros, the same as scipy's boundary="fill", which is why they match. Zero padding acts like a black frame around the picture. The box-filtered image darkens at its border, and the derivative images light up along the border with fake edges. scipy also offers "symm" (mirror the edge pixels) and "wrap", which avoid that artifact. I used "symm" in the later parts for that reason.

1.2 Finite Difference Operator

With Dx = [1 −1] and Dy = [1 −1]T, convolving the cameraman image gives the change in brightness horizontally and vertically. Dx responds to vertical edges (like the tripod legs) and Dy to horizontal ones. The gradient magnitude √(dx² + dy²) combines them into one "how much is changing here" image, which doesn't depend on edge direction.

To get an edge image, I kept pixels whose magnitude is above a threshold. Too low and the grass turns into speckle; too high and the building outlines and parts of the coat disappear. I chose 0.25 (on a 0–1 image): the grass noise is mostly gone while the man, camera, and tripod stay connected. I accepted losing some faint background buildings to keep the noise down.

1.3 Derivative of Gaussian (DoG) Filter

The finite-difference edges are noisy because the derivative amplifies pixel-level noise. Blurring first with a Gaussian (σ = 1.5, built with cv2.getGaussianKernel and an outer product) removes that noise before differentiating.

Differences from 1.2: the edges are smoother, thicker, and continuous, and the grass speckle is almost gone. Because smoothing lowers every gradient value, the threshold drops to 0.07. The tradeoff is that very fine detail and exact edge position get slightly blurred.

Bells & Whistles: Gradient Orientation

At each pixel, the gradient (dx, dy) points in the direction brightness increases fastest. I computed its angle as θ = atan2(−dy, dx) (dy is negated because image rows count downward) and mapped θ from [−π, π] onto hue in [0, 1]. Hue is circular, so angles just either side of ±π get the same color, which is the right behavior for a direction. Saturation is 1, and brightness is the gradient magnitude, so flat regions stay dark and only real edges are colored. The two sides of a thin object (like a tripod leg) get opposite colors, because the gradient points in opposite directions across it.

Part 2: Fun with Frequencies

2.1 Image "Sharpening"

A Gaussian blur keeps only the low frequencies. Subtracting it from the original leaves the high frequencies: edges and texture. Adding a multiple α of those back makes the image look sharper:

sharpened = f + α(f − f * G) = f * ((1 + α)δ − αG)

where δ is the unit impulse. The right side is a single filter, the unsharp mask, so sharpening takes one convolution. I checked that the single filter matches the two-step version to floating-point precision. I used σ = 2 and α = 1.5.

Varying the amount. Small α gives a subtle crispness. As α grows, edges get stronger, but eventually bright and dark halos appear along edges and noise is amplified.

2.2 Hybrid Images

Up close, high frequencies dominate what we see; from far away only the low frequencies are visible. A hybrid image adds the low frequencies of one image (a Gaussian blur, σlow) to the high frequencies of another (the image minus its Gaussian blur, σhigh). I aligned each pair by clicking two matching points (the eyes) and warping the second image with a similarity transform.

Derek + Nutmeg: full process

In the Fourier plots, the low-passed image keeps only a bright blob at the center (low frequencies), while the high-passed image has its center removed and keeps the outer spread. The hybrid's spectrum is the two combined.

Cutoff frequencies. I used σlow = 6 and σhigh = 3. A Gaussian with standard deviation σ falls to half strength at about 0.19/σ cycles per pixel, so Derek keeps frequencies below roughly 0.03 cycles/pixel and Nutmeg keeps those above roughly 0.06. I chose them by trying several values: with a smaller σlow, Derek was visible up close; with a larger σhigh, the cat's fur made Derek hard to see even from far away. Bells & Whistles: color

Color works best in the low-frequency image. Color lives mostly in large, smooth regions, which is exactly what a low-pass keeps, so it strengthens the far-away interpretation. In the high-pass image, color survives only as thin fringes along edges, which adds little and can look noisy. Using both gives the richest image, but the low-frequency color tends to take over and makes the close-up image harder to see. [Confirm this matches what you see.]

2.3 Gaussian and Laplacian Stacks

A stack is like a pyramid without downsampling: every level stays full size. Each Gaussian level blurs the previous one, with σ doubling each level (2, 4, 8, …) to mimic a pyramid halving in size. Each Laplacian level is the difference of two neighboring Gaussian levels, a band of frequencies. The last Laplacian level is the final Gaussian level, so adding all Laplacian levels rebuilds the original exactly. No built-in pyramid functions are used.

2.4 Multiresolution Blending

Each frequency band is blended with a matching blur of the mask: blend = Σi GMi·LAi + (1 − GMi)·LBi. Fine details switch sharply at the seam, while coarse, smooth content is mixed over a wide transition. That hides the seam without ghosting the detail. Bells & Whistles: color

I blend each RGB channel with the same mask stacks. Color makes the result far more convincing: the orange-to-red hue shift transitions as smoothly as the brightness does, because the color differences live mostly in the low-frequency bands, which get the widest, gentlest transition.
